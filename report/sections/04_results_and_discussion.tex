\section{Prediction from single cells with bulk labels}
\label{sec:results-q1}
\label{sec:rd-q1}

The first question was whether drug response can be predicted from single cells when every label is a
bulk measurement. If the expression of a line's cells carries information about its response, a per-cell
model trained on these labels should rank held-out lines within each compound better than chance and, if
single-cell resolution matters, better than a model of the line means. This was tested by scoring the six
configurations out of fold, against a ridge regression on cell-line means, against DrEval's baselines
and on the unselected catalogue, and by measuring how much of the score reflects label quality and
general cell-line fragility.

Figure~\ref{fig:panelresponse} shows the label itself, the refitted AUC of each panel compound across
the \NLinesCV{} cross-validation lines, before any model is scored. Paclitaxel, gemcitabine, dasatinib, doxorubicin and
afatinib spread widely below the no-effect level. Erlotinib, sorafenib and etoposide are centred closer to it,
and crizotinib has a narrow box well below it. Imatinib and cisplatin sit close to the no-effect level, and
cisplatin has the narrowest box of the panel, so it leaves the least to rank.

\paragraph{Discussion} The answer to the first question is a qualified yes: drug response is
predictable from these data, but modestly, unevenly across compounds, and not demonstrably from single
cells. The score is out of fold over held-out
lines, stable across seeds, and clears DrEval's naive predictors on a protocol this work did not design
\cite{dreval}. Against that, a ridge regression on cell-line means scores about as well as the
default per-cell configuration, so
the single-cell resolution and the network are not what does the work. On the unselected catalogue the signal nearly vanishes, which marks the
panel's score as a property of eleven well-covered compounds rather than of the screen.

The spread across compounds is explained on the label side: the share
of flat curves predicts the per-compound score without recourse to biology, which agrees with the known
inconsistency of pharmacogenomic labels \cite{haibekains2013,safikhani2017,bayer2023curvecurator}. With one label in nine
carrying no dose--response and roughly \NLinesCV{} independent lines \cite{lazic2010,dreval}, the binding constraint is the
supply and quality of labels, not model capacity. The relation is a trend over eleven points,
and crizotinib shows it is not a law. Cisplatin's position at the bottom was anticipated by the
literature on its determinant: only one of four ERCC1 isoforms confers repair
capacity and cisplatin resistance, and no antibody distinguishes them \cite{friboulet_ercc1}, so a
gene-level measurement cannot see the functional isoform.

About a fifth of what either representation ranks is
general cell-line fragility, in nearly the same proportion for both. Under LPO, where a
baseline can use a line's average response to the other compounds, that baseline alone reached
\LpoLineMean{}, above the \SpBest{} of the best configuration here. The two protocols hold out different
pairs and aggregate them differently, so this is a reference for the size of the channel rather than a
comparison of models. The LCO protocol used here gives no baseline access to the channel, because a held-out line has no
estimated effect \cite{dreval}. The reported score is therefore real but partly not compound-specific. What this shared component is
was not measured.

DrEval found that about half of the tested models did not significantly outperform a
predictor of mean drug and cell-line effects, and that under leave-cell-line-out evaluation no model
surpassed a tuned random forest \cite{dreval}. Here the per-cell model scored \DrMlpNorm{} against
\DrRfPcaNorm{} for DrEval's per-compound random forest on the same features. That forest
used DrEval's default hyperparameters rather than being tuned, and on all \NPca{} components, most of
which carried no signal for it, it fell far below the same forest on the first 10 components.
% (\code{dreval/dreval\_rf\_checks.csv}, \code{scripts/evaluation/dreval\_rf\_checks.py})
This comparison therefore does not test DrEval's finding that no model surpassed a tuned forest under
LCO. The result agrees with the expectation that more
complex models need more observations \cite{adam2020}, and with a benchmark on GDSC in which elastic
nets outperformed neural networks for most compounds \cite{eckhart2024}. With about \NLinesCV{} labelled lines, a ridge
regression on line means scored about as well as the network's default configuration. This differs
from the NCI-DREAM challenge, whose best methods modelled non-linear relationships \cite{costello2014}. The settings differ, however. The challenge combined
several bulk profiling data types and pathway information on breast cancer lines, and this work used
one single-cell representation without either. The closest comparison is PERCEPTION, which used the
same atlas \cite{perception}. It obtained predictive models for 44 of 133 drugs, and the drugs with
predictive models had a higher variance in response, which is compatible with the finding here that
compounds with more flat curves scored lower. Its models built on single-cell expression alone, without
pretraining on bulk data, were weaker than its bulk-pretrained ones. The per-cell model here was
trained in that weaker setting, which is one possible reason for its modest score that this work did
not test. The scores themselves are not comparable, because the screen, the response measure, the
metric and the drugs differ.

That the score is modest and uneven across compounds is in line with the size of the label set and with
the known inconsistency of drug-screen labels. Less in line with the premise of this work is that a ridge
regression on line means scores about as well as the per-cell model, so single-cell resolution shows no
measured advantage here, and that the signal nearly vanishes on the unselected catalogue.

The answer rests on about \NLinesCV{} independent labels (Section~\ref{sec:data}) \cite{lazic2010}, on one
database, one response score and \NPanel{} compounds. Cross-database integration, toxicity and
leave-tissue-out or leave-drug-out evaluation \cite{dreval} were not attempted. Three further steps would
make the answer more conclusive. Retraining under an LPO split would show whether the model clears a
baseline that can use a line's average response, which no baseline can do under LCO \cite{dreval}.
Weighting each label by the quality of its fit, for example by the $p$-value or $F$-statistic that
CurveCurator reports per curve \cite{bayer2023curvecurator}, alongside the density weight, would let the
model discount the one label in nine that comes from a flat curve. CurveCurator reports no per-curve
variance, so an inverse-variance weight would first need one. Measuring each line's
division rate would test whether the fragility component reflects proliferation, which the AUC depends on
through the number of divisions during the assay \cite{hafner}.

\section{scGPT against PCA}
\label{sec:results-q2}
\label{sec:rd-q2}

The second question was whether scGPT embeddings are a viable representation for this task, measured
against PCA at matched width. The premise of the foundation-model approach is that a pretrained embedding
is the better input (Section~\ref{sec:bg-foundation}). If it held here, the scGPT arm should score at
least as high as the PCA arm. This was tested by comparing the two arms on the trained models and on
their inputs, and by testing five alternative explanations of a difference between them.

The PCA arm scored higher than the scGPT arm in all six configurations (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}), under
all four scoring conventions --- cell or line level, pooled or per fold
% (\code{panel\_aggregation\_comparison.csv})
--- and after fragility was controlled out. On DrEval's LCO protocol the scGPT arm also scored below
the PCA arm, \DrMlpScgptNorm{} against \DrMlpNorm{}, and below DrEval's forest on the scGPT features
(\DrRfNorm{}). On the inputs
alone, before training, the between-line share of variance, one minus the within-line share defined in
Appendix~\ref{app:milcrit}, was \BtwSharePca{} for PCA and
\BtwShareScgpt{} for scGPT, a between-to-within ratio of \BtwRatioPca{} against \BtwRatioScgpt{}.
For PCA this used one decomposition fitted to the cells of all \NLinesCV{} cross-validation lines, in
place of the five per-fold fits the model receives.

% test lines included, until 21.10.2026)
Over a paired bootstrap of the \NLinesCV{} lines (Appendix~\ref{app:milcrit}), the difference of the
ratios was \BtwRatioDiff{} with a 95\,\% interval from \BtwRatioDiffLo{} to \BtwRatioDiffHi{}, and PCA was
ahead in \BtwBootPcaAheadPct\,\% of resamples.

\paragraph{Discussion} The answer to the second question is that scGPT is viable but falls behind
PCA here, and the leading account is a mismatch of granularity rather than a deficit of the embedding.
The label is defined per cell line, so what a model must read is variation between lines. Before any
model is trained, PCA's between-to-within variance ratio is about a fifth higher than scGPT's, which
Figure~\ref{fig:umap} illustrates. Its authors note that the current pretraining does not inherently
mitigate batch effects \cite{scgpt}, so this reduction is an observation on this atlas, not a design
property of the model. On this label it removes exactly the structure the label varies over.

Five rival accounts do not explain the gap. The margin is larger with more labels than with few, which
contradicts label starvation. It stays at the same level when PCA is refitted on a smaller atlas,
wherever the model scores above zero, which contradicts adaptation to this atlas. Capacity lowers both
arms. Rescaling the input left the epoch of best validation error almost unchanged, consistent with the
Adam update underlying AdamW, whose step magnitudes are invariant to rescaling of the gradient
\cite{kingma2015adam}, although rescaling the input is not the same as rescaling the gradient. PCA also
stays ahead once fragility is removed. Eliminating rivals does not establish the granularity account,
whose positive evidence is one variance ratio, robust to resampling the lines and illustrated by one
figure. The margins are conservative with respect to input dropout, since the
shared rate costs the PCA arm and not the scGPT arm.

PCA's lead does not come from seeing more of the data than scGPT. Both arms read the same \NHvg{} genes,
selected on expression alone and without labels, so the lines without labels could inform the selection
without leaking a response. Selecting genes per fold would have produced five gene sets, so comparisons
across folds, representations and the gene-set sweep would differ in genes as well as cells. Within each
fold, the PCA standardisation and rotation were fitted only on the cells the model itself is fitted on,
so no held-out line shaped the PCA features it was scored on. Fitting them on the whole training side
would have given PCA about \CfgExtraPct\,\% more cells than the model it feeds. The two asymmetries that
remain, the vocabulary and the fitting on this atlas, are discussed below.

The ordering agrees with the zero-shot evaluation of
Kedzierska et al., in which frozen scGPT and Geneformer embeddings separated known cell types worse
than a selection of highly variable genes (Section~\ref{sec:bg-foundation}) \cite{kedzierska2025}. Here, too, the
frozen scGPT embedding fell behind a reduction of highly variable genes, on a different task and at a
different granularity, the cell line rather than the cell type. The composition of scGPT's
pretraining corpus, which contains no cancer cell lines (Section~\ref{sec:bg-foundation}), offers a
further account that this work did not test \cite{scgpt}. Both foundation models
were designed to be fine-tuned towards downstream tasks with limited task-specific data, and scGPT's
reported results on cell type annotation, batch integration and perturbation prediction were obtained
after fine-tuning \cite{scgpt,theodoris2023}. This work therefore tests the zero-shot use of the
embedding, not the use its authors intended, which is the limitation stated below.

scGPT was chosen in the expectation that its embedding would carry less cell-line identity than PCA, an
impression the UMAP of the atlas gives (Figure~\ref{fig:umap}), and the variance ratio confirms it. What
was not expected is that this property works against it here: on a label defined per cell line, the
identity it removes is the structure the label varies over. The premise that a pretrained embedding is
the better input was therefore not borne out for this task. This is a statement about evaluation design
--- a representation that carries less cell-line identity on this atlas, scored on a label defined per
cell line --- not about scGPT in general.

scGPT was used frozen, because about \NLinesCV{} labelled lines are too few to fine-tune it and the
project's time did not allow it, so the direct test of the granularity account, fine-tuning scGPT on the
task, has not been run \cite{scgpt}. Only PCA is fitted on this atlas, so the comparison is not
like-for-like with respect to adaptation, although the atlas-size test shows that PCA's lead does not
depend on how many cells it is fitted on. PCA also decomposes every selected gene, while scGPT reads only
those in its vocabulary. The learning rate, gradient-clipping threshold, scheduler settings,
early-stopping patience and batch size of 128 cells are conventional defaults, neither sourced nor
varied, and with about a hundred fitting lines per fold the batch size may be large relative to the
number of independent labels. They were not tuned on the results, so the comparison is fair, applied
identically to both arms, but not strong. Training on MPS (Table~\ref{tab:training}) is not
bit-reproducible, and agreement between MPS and CPU embeddings of the same cells was not verified.

\section{Heterogeneity learned by a single-cell model}
\label{sec:results-q3}
\label{sec:rd-q3}

The third question was whether a model trained on single cells learns cellular heterogeneity implicitly.
If it does, a model freed from the per-cell objective's penalty on spread within a line should give a
line's cells different predictions, and these differences should be reproducible across seeds, not
explained by sequencing quality or the cell cycle, and able to separate cells known to differ in response.
This was tested with the MIL model and the five-stage evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:mil}.

The MIL model \cite{dietterich1997,ilse2018} was run over three seeds on both representations and
evaluated by the five stages of Section~\ref{sec:mil}. The input ceiling was computed per line on the
\NLinesCV{} lines. The spread, reproducibility and confound stages read only predictions, so they were
computed on all \QPairs{} (compound, line) combinations of the panel, including the \QUnscreened{} that
were not screened, since a held-out line is predicted without its labels either way. The positive control
used \QBags{} mixed two-line bags per seed.
% (until 21.10.2026 every stage was said to run on the \QPairs{} pairs; stage 0 counts lines, stage 7 bags)
The positive control is reported last because it calibrates the others. The first stage,
the input ceiling, is the between-to-within ratio of Section~\ref{sec:rd-q2}, and neither representation
collapsed a line's cells onto one point.
% (\code{notebooks/4b\_mil\_training.ipynb}, \code{mil/q2\_verdict.csv})

The spread stage asked whether the MIL model gives a line's cells more different predictions than the
per-cell model, whose objective penalises exactly that spread (Equation~\ref{eq:mil-decomposition}).
It did so in \QSpreadPca\,\% of pairs for PCA and \QSpreadScgpt\,\% for scGPT, on all three seeds. For
PCA the median within-line SD of the predictions was \QSdMilPcaLo{} to \QSdMilPcaHi{} across seeds,
against \QSdCellPcaLo{} to \QSdCellPcaHi{} for the per-cell model. For scGPT it was \QSdMilScgptLo{} to
\QSdMilScgptHi{}, against \QSdCellScgptLo{} to \QSdCellScgptHi{}.
% (\code{mil/stage1\_spread\_vs\_percell.csv})

The reproducibility stage asked whether that spread is the same in independently initialised models.
Between pairs of seeds, the per-cell predictions within a line correlated with a median $\rho$ of
\QSeedRhoPca{} for PCA and \QSeedRhoScgpt{} for scGPT, and the correlation exceeded the null from
permuting cells within the line in \QSeedSigPca\,\% and \QSeedSigScgpt\,\% of pairs.
% (\code{mil/stage2\_cross\_seed\_agreement.csv})

The confound stage asked how much of that reproducible spread is explained by sequencing depth,
detected genes, mitochondrial fraction and the two cell-cycle scores. The median $R^2$ of these five
covariates was \QConfRawPca{} for PCA and \QConfRawScgpt{} for scGPT, and the adjusted $R^2$, on which the
veto is read, was \QConfPca{} and \QConfScgpt{}. Against the squared cross-seed correlation of
\QReprPca{} and \QReprScgpt{}, that is \QBarPca\,\% and \QBarScgpt\,\% of the veto bar, so the veto did not
fire for either representation. Within each line, the scGPT arm's predictions followed the
proteasomal-degradation program most closely, weakly and negatively (median within-line Spearman
$\rho = \QProgDegraScgpt$, and $\QProgDegraScgptPartial$ with mitochondrial fraction controlled), while
the program score itself barely tracked sequencing depth within a line ($\rho = \QProgDegraDepth$).
% (\code{mil/stage6\_confounds.csv})

Figure~\ref{fig:q2instrument} shows the positive control, to test whether the model can separate
two populations that are known to differ slightly in response. The median was \QAurocPca{} for PCA and
\QAurocScgpt{} for scGPT, and \QAurocSigPca\,\% and \QAurocSigScgpt\,\% of the pairs separated
significantly from the permutation null in the direction of the measured response, with the more
resistant line's cells ranked higher (one-sided test). The PCA distribution was close to uniform, whereas the scGPT distribution peaked at 0
and 1, with most values in between. The peak at 1 marks pairs separated in the direction of the
measured response, and the peak at 0 pairs separated in the opposite direction.

The MIL model was also scored like the per-cell model, on the mean prediction of each line. At the
same density exponent and loss ($\alpha = 0.5$, MSE), it scored \MilScorePca{} for PCA against
\SwPcaMseHalf{} for the per-cell model, and \MilScoreScgpt{} for scGPT against \SwScgptMseHalf{}. The bag
objective thus lowered the PCA arm and raised the scGPT arm, and the margin between the arms fell from
$+\MilMarginPercell$ to $+\MilMarginBag$. Splitting each compound's held-out lines at the median of the
per-cell model's within-line spread, the MIL model's gain over the per-cell model was \MilGapLowPca{}
in the low-spread half and \MilGapHighPca{} in the high-spread half for PCA, and \MilGapLowScgpt{} and
\MilGapHighScgpt{} for scGPT.
% (\code{panel/panel\_metrics.csv}, \code{diagnostics/mil\_by\_within\_line\_spread.csv},
% \code{scripts/evaluation/mil\_within\_line\_test.py})

\paragraph{Discussion} The third question remains open. The MIL model separates a line's cells
reproducibly and not as a sequencing artefact, but it does not reliably separate two lines known to
differ slightly: the median AUROC of its positive control is barely above chance, so the evaluation
cannot carry a negative result and makes the positive one weak. The PCA arm clears the confound veto at
\QBarPca\,\% of the bar, so its within-line signal is not confound-dominated rather than confound-free.
Whether any of the variation is drug-response heterogeneity cannot be decided here, because no per-cell
response was measured.

The association with the proteasomal-degradation program describes what the reproducible variation
follows, but it bears neither on predictive performance, because the scores are computed on line means,
nor on drug response, because the positive control did not show that the model detects known
differences in response.

The two prior methods closest to this work also evaluated
per-cell predictions through labels or outcomes defined for groups of cells. scDEAL scored its
predictions against binary labels that, in most of the original studies, were assigned to whole groups
of cells by their treatment condition \cite{scdeal}. PERCEPTION, on the same atlas, scored a line by the
mean of its cells' predictions and evaluated clone-level summaries against the response of patients
\cite{perception}. The positive control here follows the same logic, a ground truth defined for groups
of cells. Its pairs of lines were taken from the lowest quartile of each compound's pairwise response
differences (Section~\ref{sec:mil}), so it tests the smallest differences in response. Whether the
model detects larger differences was not tested.

That the MIL model spreads its predictions more than the per-cell model was expected, since it removes
the term that penalises the spread (Equation~\ref{eq:mil-decomposition}). Less expected was that the bag
objective lowered the PCA arm and raised the scGPT arm. One account was that a per-cell objective treats
within-line variation as noise, that scGPT carries more of it, and that pooling into a bag removes
exactly that. It predicted a larger gain where the within-line spread is larger, but the gain was
smaller in the high-spread half for both arms, so the account is refuted, and why the bag objective moves
the arms in opposite directions remains open.

A negative answer to the third question would be uninformative, and little exists to validate a positive
one. The only response measurement below the line level for this atlas is the source study's screen of
sorted subpopulations, which found that co-existing subpopulations differ in drug sensitivity but covers
two head and neck cancer lines \cite{scp542}. Post-treatment single-cell profiles exist for other lines
and compounds \cite{srivatsan2020,tahoe100m} but carry no per-cell response label. A conclusive answer
would need per-cell or per-subpopulation response measurements on the lines modelled, and a positive
control over larger differences in response than the lowest quartile tested here.
